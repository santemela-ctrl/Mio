# 2.3 Executive powers and the reservation of law in digital security

The study of the institutional architecture just carried out helps to gain a better understanding of Italian cyber governance, which has progressively concentrated the powers of direction, regulation, supervision and crisis response at the apex of the Executive. The President of the Council of Ministers adopts the national strategy and the regulations governing the Agency; the Agency contributes to drafting the rules, oversees their application and sanctions their breach; the entities of the Perimeter are identified by an act that is neither published nor accessible; and the intelligence system retains the most incisive powers of intervention, from the deactivation of products to cyber countermeasures. Having established *who* holds these powers, it is now necessary to ask *how* they are conferred: through which normative instruments, and within which constitutional limits, does the Executive act in the digital domain?

In the Italian constitutional order, the first answer lies in the reservation of law (*riserva di legge*), the rule whereby certain matters, and above all restrictions on fundamental freedoms, may be regulated only by an act of Parliament or by an act having the force of law, so that the decision on how far a freedom can be compressed is taken by the representative assembly, through a public procedure, and remains subject to review by the Constitutional Court. The Constitution graduates the intensity of this guarantee. Personal liberty, the inviolability of the home and the freedom and secrecy of correspondence and of every other form of communication are protected by a double reservation, of law and of jurisdiction: restrictions are admissible only in the cases and in the manner provided for by law and, as a rule, by means of a reasoned act of the judicial authority.[^97] Personal and patrimonial obligations, as well as the controls to which economic initiative may be subjected, are instead covered by a relative reservation, which is satisfied when the statute lays down the essential elements of the discipline and the criteria circumscribing administrative discretion.[^98] Digital security touches both levels. The duties imposed on the operators of the Perimeter and on the entities subject to the NIS rules restrict economic freedom and impose obligations on private parties, whereas the intrusion of public authorities into devices, networks and communications affects the freedoms protected by articles 14 and 15 of the Constitution, as well as a right to digital confidentiality (*riservatezza informatica*) which, beyond the protection it receives from European sources, could be traced back to article 2.[^99]

The argument developed here is that, in the cyber domain, the reservation of law is formally respected but substantively weakened along three lines: the sources through which powers are conferred, the content of the powers themselves and the technical instruments through which they are exercised. This diagnosis is consistent with the observation that the regulatory proliferation of recent years has produced an extremely vast and varied system architecture, generating inevitable difficulties of application, and with the suggestion that such difficulties should be overcome by starting again from respect for the hierarchy of sources.[^100]

The first line concerns the sources. The Italian cyber architecture was not born of a statute. Its first comprehensive design was contained in a directive adopted by decree of the President of the Council of Ministers (d.P.C.M.) on 24 January 2013, the so-called Monti decree, which outlined the institutional architecture responsible for national security with particular regard to cyber protection, distributing closely complementary tasks among the President of the Council, the Interministerial Committee for the Security of the Republic (CISR), the Department of Information for Security (DIS) and the Cyber Security Unit; it was replaced by the d.P.C.M. of 17 February 2017, the so-called Gentiloni decree, which redesigned the organisational structure and was accompanied by a new National Plan.[^101] The statutory basis for these acts was essentially the provision, introduced in 2012 into the law on the intelligence system, empowering the President of the Council to issue directives to the DIS and to the intelligence agencies in order to strengthen information activities for the protection of critical infrastructures, with particular regard to cyber protection and national information security.[^102] A norm conferring a power of direction over the intelligence services thus became the foundation of an entire public policy which, as seen in § 2.1, extends far beyond intelligence; and, as has been observed, the first intervention of primary rank specifically devoted to cybersecurity came only in 2018, with the legislative decree implementing the NIS directive.[^103]

Even when primary sources arrived, however, they were for the most part acts of the Executive. The legislative decree (d.lgs.) n. 65 of 2018 was adopted by the Government under a parliamentary delegation (article 76 of the Constitution), while the Perimeter and the Agency were established by decree-laws (d.l.), that is, by provisional measures that the Government adopts “under its own responsibility” in “extraordinary cases of necessity and urgency”, and which lose effect from the outset unless converted into law within sixty days (article 77). The same instrument has been used for several subsequent adjustments, the main exception being Law n. 90 of 2024, while the NIS 2 directive was once again transposed by legislative decree.[^104] The obligation for public administrations to diversify their cybersecurity products away from suppliers linked to the Russian Federation was introduced by a decree-law adopted after the invasion of Ukraine and specified by a circular of the Agency;[^105] the power of the President of the Council to order intelligence countermeasures in cyberspace was inserted by a decree-law whose title refers to energy, the water emergency and social and industrial policies;[^106] and the special powers over 5G networks, later extended to cloud technologies, were also introduced by decree-laws.[^107] Below the primary level, the regulatory density lies in the d.P.C.M.s implementing the Perimeter, in the regulations on the Agency, adopted in derogation from the ordinary procedure, and even in the allocation of the resources for the national strategy, which the budget law entrusts to a decree of the President of the Council.[^108] The pattern has recently been confirmed at the highest level: the management of crises involving national security, including those arising from cyber and hybrid threats, and the procedure for adopting the first National Security Strategy have been regulated by a d.P.C.M. of April 2026.[^109]

The result is a peculiar circularity. The Executive writes the statute, through decree-laws and legislative decrees; it writes the rules implementing it, through d.P.C.M.s and regulations; it applies them, through the Agency; and, in the most sensitive cases, it withholds the relevant acts from publication and access. Parliament is not absent, but it intervenes mostly *ex post*, when converting decree-laws or delivering opinions on draft decrees, and through a Committee that operates under conditions of confidentiality (§ 2.1).

Whether this configuration infringes the reservation of law depends, as regards the obligations imposed on private operators, on the constitutional case law on relative reservations. The Constitutional Court has held that the principle of legality in its “substantive” sense requires the statute that attributes a power also to define its content, without leaving the administration an unlimited discretion;[^110] it has also accepted, however, that the criteria limiting discretion may be inferred from the composition and functioning of the competent bodies, or from a procedure involving the collaboration of several bodies, capable of excluding arbitrariness.[^111] The Perimeter relies precisely on this second path: the only statutory criterion is gradualness (§ 2.2), while the real guarantee is organisational, entrusted to the interplay between the Ministries, the Interministerial Committee for Cybersecurity, the Agency and the President of the Council. Yet a procedure of this kind can compensate for the absence of statutory criteria only if it brings together perspectives capable of checking one another; when all the bodies involved belong to the Executive and the final act is not accessible, the organisational guarantee risks becoming self-referential. Fragmentation, therefore, is not merely a problem of efficiency: the lack of a unitary reading of obligations and duties, which the literature regards as a weakness of the European framework itself, is also a problem of legality, since obligations that are difficult to reconstruct are also difficult to foresee and to challenge.[^112]

The second line concerns the content of the powers, and it is most visible in the emergency powers. The power to order the total or partial deactivation of apparatuses and products, under article 5 of the d.l. n. 105 of 2019, is defined not by its object but by its conditions and limits: a serious and imminent risk to national security connected to the vulnerability of networks, systems and services, or a situation of cyber crisis; a resolution of the CISR; indispensability; the time strictly necessary to eliminate or mitigate the specific risk factor; and proportionality.[^113] Likewise, the countermeasures provided for by article 7-*ter* of the d.l. n. 174 of 2015 may be ordered in situations of crisis or emergency, in the face of threats to national security that cannot be addressed by resilience actions alone, and are carried out by the intelligence agencies, which may also rely on the functional guarantees afforded to their personnel.[^114] In both cases the reservation of law is satisfied by a norm of competence and purpose, which identifies who decides and why, but not what may be done: the content of a “countermeasure” in cyberspace, potentially an offensive operation against networks and systems, is left to dispositions adopted within the intelligence system, of which Parliament learns only through the Parliamentary Committee for the Security of the Republic (COPASIR). The structure recalls that of extraordinary ordinance powers, which constitutional case law admits precisely because they are tied to urgency and to the temporariness of their effects, while denying the same derogatory capacity to ordinary administrative powers.[^115] In the cyber domain, however, emergency is not an exception to an otherwise defined framework, but the very condition in which the power is designed to operate, and its triggering concept, national security, is the most indeterminate of all. The European Court of Human Rights has warned against precisely this risk: it would be contrary to the rule of law for a discretion granted to the executive in the sphere of national security to be expressed in terms of unfettered power, and a law allowing surveillance on account of threats to “national, military, economic or ecological security”, without defining them, leaves the authorities an almost unlimited discretion.[^116] That the conditions for activating the countermeasures have not so far arisen does not diminish the problem, since the quality of a law is measured by what it allows, not by the use made of it to date.[^117]

The third line concerns the instruments, and it is here that the tension between executive power and the reservation of law has emerged most clearly. The paradigmatic case is the *captatore informatico*, or State trojan: a malware covertly installed on a target device, which enables a remote command centre to take over the device and to carry out both online searches, copying all or part of the stored data, and online surveillance, monitoring incoming and outgoing information flows and capturing what appears on the screen, what is typed on the keyboard and the images and sounds within range of the device.[^118] Its spread reflects the shift of criminal investigations from repression to prevention and, above all, the diffusion of end-to-end encryption, which can be circumvented only by capturing communications on the device, before they are encrypted.[^119] Yet government hacking has developed, in most States, in a regulatory limbo: apart from Germany, which formally authorised forms of covert capture as early as 2006, several European States introduced specific legislative provisions only from 2014 onwards, and many rely on “grey area legal provisions”, designed for similar investigative measures and applied by analogy; the frameworks governing intelligence services are even thinner, since the culture of secrecy that characterises their activity entails a poor definition of their powers, inadequate safeguards and the lack of solid independent oversight.[^120]

The legal problem is compounded by a security problem, which makes the trojan particularly relevant to a study on cybersecurity. Hacking tools are produced and supplied by private companies, to which capture operations are frequently outsourced; the attack suffered in 2015 by the Italian company Hacking Team, which led to the online publication of the source code of its intrusion software, showed that such tools can be turned against the very security they are meant to protect.[^121] Moreover, techniques designed to defeat the security measures of operating systems make devices more vulnerable to subsequent attacks, weakening entire information systems and strategic infrastructures.[^122] For this reason, the study commissioned by the Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs (LIBE) recommended that the European Parliament call on Member States to adopt a clear and precise legal basis before law enforcement agencies resort to hacking, and to carry out a privacy impact assessment whenever new laws on the matter are proposed.[^123] The reservation of law thus protects not only the person targeted, but also the security of the digital ecosystem as a whole, which the literature has come to regard as a public good.[^124]

Italy offers a telling example of how such a tool can enter practice before, and outside, the statute. For more than a decade the trojan was used by investigators and intelligence services within a patchy and unclear legal framework, in which the inertia of the legislator was compensated for by the courts, which qualified it as an atypical means of evidence gathering, under article 189 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, or assimilated each of its functions to inspections, searches and interceptions.[^125] In 2016 the Joint Sections of the Court of Cassation, in the *Scurato* judgment, established some fixed points, but only with regard to one of the tool’s capabilities, the interception of conversations between persons present through a trojan installed on portable devices, which they allowed only in proceedings for organised crime.[^126] It was therefore a court, not Parliament, that first defined the conditions of use of the most intrusive investigative instrument available to the State; and it was an appeal drafted by scholars of criminal procedure and signed by more than seventy academics, together with the United Nations Human Rights Committee, which in 2017 recommended that Italy “review the regime regulating the interception of personal communications, hacking of digital devices and the retention of communications data” and involve the judiciary in the authorisation of such measures, that denounced the regulatory vacuum.[^127]

The subsequent discipline confirms the prevalence of executive sources. Parliament delegated the matter to the Government, which regulated it by legislative decree, typifying solely the use of the trojan as a remote microphone on portable devices, and only in criminal proceedings, leaving aside the intelligence services, and referring the technical requirements of the software to a decree of the Minister of Justice, adopted in April 2018 after an opinion of the Italian Data Protection Authority (*Garante per la protezione dei dati personali*).[^128] The entry into force of the reform was repeatedly postponed, by decree-laws and even by the budget law, while the so-called *spazza-corrotti* law extended the use of the trojan to the most serious offences committed by public officials against the public administration: a cross-play of normative interventions of opposite sign which, in Graziani’s words, produced a “*pasticcio*” of doubtful conformity with European case law.[^129] The discipline finally became operational through yet another decree-law, for proceedings registered after 31 August 2020, and was revised in 2023, once again through a decree-law and its conversion law, so as to require the judge to state, on the basis of an autonomous assessment, the specific reasons that make the use of the trojan concretely necessary.[^130] In the meantime, the risks feared by scholars had materialised: in the *Exodus* case, a spyware used for interceptions was distributed through applications downloadable by anyone, and data of persons unrelated to any investigation would have been transferred to cloud servers located in the United States, prompting the Garante to address a report to Parliament and to the Government and to call for an organic reform.[^131]

Two conclusions can be drawn. First, the discipline of the trojan has been shaped almost entirely by acts of the Executive and by case law, with Parliament in a condition that Graziani describes as one of “aphonia”.[^132] Second, the scope of the statutory discipline remains narrower than the capabilities of the instrument: the functions other than the interception of conversations, such as the extraction of stored data, screenshots and keylogging, still lack a specific discipline and are left to judicial reconstruction, contrary to the principle that restrictions of fundamental rights are admissible only within a rigorously determined normative framework, and at a time when the courts have not been able to contain the recourse to interceptions, which in 2017 involved more than 127,000 targets.[^133]

European standards help to measure the gap. For the European Court of Human Rights, an interference with private life must be “in accordance with the law”, but the notion of law is material rather than formal: it includes secondary sources and case law, and what matters is not the origin of the norm but its quality, that is, its accessibility, clarity and foreseeability.[^134] Since secret surveillance escapes the scrutiny of those concerned and of the public, the law must indicate the scope of any discretion conferred on the authorities and the manner of its exercise with sufficient clarity, and must lay down minimum safeguards concerning the nature of the offences, the categories of persons, the duration of the measures, the procedures for examining, using and storing the data, the precautions for their communication to other parties and the circumstances of their destruction.[^135] Moreover, since a system of secret surveillance “may undermine or even destroy democracy under the cloak of defending it”, adequate and effective guarantees against abuse are required, including authorisation and supervision by bodies independent of the executive[^136] and, for bulk interception, “end-to-end safeguards”.[^137] The Court of Justice has reached analogous conclusions under the Charter of Fundamental Rights, requiring that access to retained data be subject to prior review by a court or an independent administrative body which, in criminal matters, must not be involved in the investigation;[^138] significantly, Italy adapted its rules on the acquisition of telephone and internet records by introducing the authorisation of the judge, so that the reservation of jurisdiction came from Luxembourg, while the instrument, once again, was a decree-law.[^139] The German Federal Constitutional Court went further, deriving from the general right of personality a fundamental right to the confidentiality and integrity of information technology systems, and making covert access to them conditional upon factual indications of a concrete danger to an overriding legal interest, a statutory basis ensuring certainty and determinacy, strict proportionality, technical measures protecting the core area of private life and independent authorisation.[^140]

The comparison reveals the complementarity, and the respective limits, of the two notions of legality. The Italian reservation is formal and hierarchical: it ensures that certain choices are made by Parliament, but says little about the precision of the resulting rules. The European notion is material and qualitative: it tolerates secondary sources and case law, but demands clarity, foreseeability and safeguards. In the digital domain the Italian framework risks failing both tests, since the provenance of its rules is predominantly executive and their content, where emergency powers and intrusive tools are concerned, is often indeterminate. The Constitutional Court has nonetheless laid the foundations for a more demanding reading: by holding that e-mails and instant messages are “correspondence” protected by article 15 of the Constitution even after they have been received and read, it has extended the double reservation of law and jurisdiction to the messages stored in digital devices, which are among the first things a trojan can extract.[^141] Although the Constitution does not recognise, as such, a right to the integrity of information systems, the combined reading of articles 2, 14 and 15, in the light of European standards, points in the same direction.

The area in which the reservation of law remains most fragile is intelligence. The 2017 reform did not regulate hacking by the security services, although the secrecy of their activity cannot justify a departure from the principle of legality, as the Strasbourg case law on surveillance by security agencies, from *Klass* to *Big Brother Watch*, consistently shows.[^142] Preventive interceptions by the agencies are authorised by the Prosecutor General at the Court of Appeal of Rome, a public prosecutor rather than a judge, and their discipline was substantially amended by a provision of the 2023 budget law.[^143] The *Paragon* affair has shown the practical relevance of the issue: in June 2025 the COPASIR reported that the external intelligence agency had used a commercial spyware within preventive interceptions authorised under that procedure and concluded that the services had acted in compliance with the Constitution and the law, while acknowledging that the evolution of cyber-surveillance technologies calls for an adaptation of the legal framework.[^144] The case confirms, six years later, the concern that the intelligence use of trojans rests on rules conceived for traditional interceptions, and that parliamentary scrutiny of it takes place only in the confidential forum of the COPASIR.

These findings also shed light on the relationship between cybersecurity and digital sovereignty. Digital sovereignty is still largely understood as a discursive political practice rather than as a legal and normative concept,[^145] and the legislation on the Perimeter has itself been criticised by part of the literature as an expression of “sovereigntism”.[^146] The reservation of law is precisely the instrument that allows sovereignty in cyberspace to be exercised as a constitutional power rather than as a mere act of force. Cybersecurity and digital sovereignty find their ultimate legitimation in the capacity to guarantee the security of data and information in full compliance with the fundamental rights enshrined in higher-ranking norms, and it is only through the synergy between the two that a unitary body of rules, capable of supporting and informing State action on the basis of shared democratic principles, can be envisaged.[^147] In concrete terms, this would require a framework statute that defines with sufficient precision the conditions, content and duration of emergency powers; that typifies all the functions of intrusive tools, including those used by the intelligence services; that provides for independent authorisation and *ex post* review; and that restores to Parliament, in public form, a role going beyond the conversion of decree-laws and the confidential oversight of the COPASIR. How legality, proportionality and precaution can operate as limits on these powers, and what role the Garante can play as the only independent guarantor within the system, are the questions addressed in the next chapter.[^148]
